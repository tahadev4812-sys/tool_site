How to use this calculator

  1. Pick your units and measure the river: its length, average width and the depth you will pour.
  2. Choose how many rivers run through the table (1–3). If you have two, enter the average width of each.
  3. Enter the maximum pour depth from your epoxy’s data sheet — typically 2 in (5 cm) for deep-pour epoxy.
  4. Switch on the flood coat if you plan to coat the whole top, and add the full tabletop width.
  5. Press Calculate to see the total volume, resin and hardener amounts and the number of layers.

The formula

river volume = length × avg river width × depth × rivers flood coat = length × table width × coat thickness total = (river + flood) × (1 + waste% ÷ 100) layers = ceil( depth ÷ max pour depth ) // in³ → gallons ÷ 231 · fl oz ÷ 1.805 · liters ÷ 61.024
Worked exampleA 72 in long table with one 6 in wide river poured 2 in deep: 72 × 6 × 2 = 864 in³. A 1/8 in flood coat over a 36 in wide top adds 72 × 36 × 0.125 = 324 in³. Together that is 1,188 in³; with 10% waste = 1,306.8 in³, which is about 5.7 gallons. Depth 2 in ÷ max pour 2 in = 1 layer.

Why river tables use so much epoxy

Volume grows quickly with depth. A river that is 6 inches wide and 2 inches deep along a 6 ft table holds about 3.7 gallons — and wide, curving rivers with irregular edges often hold more than the straight-line maths suggests. That is why measuring the average width matters: measure the river at the narrowest point, the widest point and a few places between, then average the numbers.

Pouring deep without cracking

Deep-pour epoxy is formulated to release heat slowly, but even the best products have a maximum thickness per pour. If your slabs are 3 inches thick and your epoxy tops out at 2 inches, the calculator will recommend two layers. For best results, pour the second layer while the first is still tacky so the layers chemically bond. Waiting until the first layer has fully cured means sanding between coats.

  • Keep the shop at the temperature stated on the data sheet.
  • Warm the resin bottles in a water bath on cold days to reduce bubbles.
  • Seal the wood with a thin coat first so air does not escape into the river.

Do I need a flood coat?

A flood coat is a thin layer of epoxy poured over the whole tabletop after the river has cured and been sanded flat. It protects the wood, deepens the grain and gives a glass-like finish. At 1/8 inch it adds about 10 fl oz per square foot, so budget for it separately when buying epoxy.

Frequently asked questions

How much epoxy do I need for a river table?

Multiply river length × average river width × depth to get the volume in cubic inches, then divide by 231 for US gallons. A 6 ft table with a 6 in wide river poured 2 in deep needs about 3.7 gallons plus waste.

How deep can I pour a river table in one go?

It depends on the epoxy. Deep-pour formulas are commonly rated for 2 in per pour; some go to 3 in or more. Tabletop epoxy is usually limited to around 1/8–1/2 in. Always follow the manufacturer’s data sheet.

What is a flood coat on a river table?

A thin, final layer of epoxy over the entire tabletop that seals the wood and gives a smooth, glossy finish. It is typically about 1/8 inch thick.

Should I use a 1:1 or 2:1 epoxy?

Both work. 2:1 deep-pour epoxies are popular because they react more slowly and generate less heat. The ratio affects how much of each part you buy, not the total volume.

How much extra epoxy should I mix?

10% is typical, but go to 15–20% for live-edge slabs with cracks, voids or porous wood because the epoxy fills them first.

Why does my river table keep leaking?

Gaps between the mold and the slabs let low-viscosity epoxy escape. Seal every seam with tape and silicone before you pour, and test with a small amount first.

Can I use this for a bar top or charcuterie board?

Yes. Enter the dimensions of the area that will be filled with epoxy and set the number of rivers to one. For a bar top coat, use the flood coat option.
